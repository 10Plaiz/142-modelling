| **Working title** | Capacity of the single-reader vehicle entrance at the Mapúa University Makati campus parking lot |
| **Group number** | 8 |
| **Members (full names)** | Ariola, Jon Luigi Bauza, Xyrelle John Caliliw, Marl Zeldrix Jimenez, Justine Leee |
| **Date** | 14 September 2026 (revised 30 September 2026) |
| **The system in one line** | Cars arrive at the campus parking entrance and queue in one lane on the approach. At a single stop, a guard checks the parking sticker and the driver taps a University ID at the one reader. The barrier lifts and the car enters; a car without a sticker is refused and turns out of the lane. |
| **Part 1: problem and decision** | The entrance has one stop position, one ID reader and one guard from 07:00 to 19:00. Morning arrivals are concentrated before classes, and the approach can hold only a few cars before the tail of the line reaches Pablo Ocampo Sr. Extension, where it would block through traffic. Group members have **not** seen the line reach the street. The study does not assume a problem; it measures how much margin the gate has before there would be one. ***Decision questions:*** on a high-traffic day, (1) is one guard enough? (2) How much must traffic grow before the line reaches the street on at least one day in ten? (3) Does a second guard from 07:00 to 09:00, or from 07:00 to 10:00, buy enough extra capacity to justify its 2 or 3 extra guard-hours? (4) Do these answers hold when arrival demand is 20% lower or higher than estimated? The second guard shares the one reader, so it cannot open a second lane. It can only pre-check the sticker of the next car while the car ahead taps. The model estimates the queueing effect. Whether that effect is worth a guard's wage is a management judgement outside the model. ***Why simulation:*** demand rises and falls within the morning, so the line never settles into the steady state that closed-form queueing formulas assume. The pre-check arrangement is a two-station line with blocking, which has no simple formula. And trialling a second guard at the real gate is not possible for the group. |
| **Part 2: entities** | Cars arriving to enter the lot. One car is one entity, for example a faculty car that arrives at 07:42, waits behind three others, has its sticker checked, taps an ID and enters. Attributes: arrival time; sticker or no sticker; its own sticker-check time C, ID-tap time T, move-up time M and, for a no-sticker car, refusal time R. |
| **Part 2: resources** | The **stop** with the one ID reader (one car at a time). The **pre-check spot** one car-length upstream (one car at a time, used only while guard 2 is on duty). **Guard 1**, 07:00–19:00. **Guard 2**, only in the alternative configurations. ***Configurations:*** A, one guard (12 guard-hours); B, second guard 07:00–09:00 (14); C, second guard 07:00–10:00 (15). |
| **Part 2: arrivals** | Cars arrive one at a time across the 12-hour operating day. Arrivals are modelled as a Poisson process whose rate is constant within each 15-minute interval, so the morning peak is represented by higher rates in those intervals. |
| **Part 2: run start and stop** | A run is one operating day, 07:00 to 19:00. Arrivals stop at 19:00; cars already on the approach are processed before the run ends. Each run starts with an empty line and idle guards. This is a **terminating** study: the day has a natural start and end, and the empty 07:00 start is part of the question, so no warm-up period is discarded. |
| **Part 2: out of scope** | 1\. Finding a space inside the two-level basement: every car that passes the barrier is assumed to find one. 2\. Exit traffic, which leaves without a check and does not use the guard. 3\. Balking and reneging: every arriving car joins the line and stays until processed or refused. 4\. Reader breakdowns (only a slow reader is tested, in Part 9). |
| **Part 3: model type** | Discrete-event simulation in the process-interaction world view (SimPy maintains the future event list). A continuous fluid-flow model is built alongside it, only as a cross-check. Monte Carlo estimation is used for the probability that a day has spillover. |
| **Part 3: why this type fits** | ***Classification:*** the model is **dynamic**, because the order and timing of arrivals determine each car's delay. It is **stochastic**, because interarrival and activity times vary, so the same configuration gives different days on different runs. It is **discrete**, because its state changes only at events: the number of cars on the approach, and the busy-or-idle status of the stop, the pre-check spot and each guard. ***Events:*** arrival; reaches the pre-check spot; pre-check ends; reaches the stop; sticker check ends; refused; enters the lot; guard 2 goes off duty (09:00 or 10:00). ***Activity vs delay:*** C, T, M and R are activities the model samples. A car's delay (time in system minus its own activity time) is produced as output and never sampled. ***Monte Carlo element:*** each replication is one sampled high-traffic day. The share of simulated days with any spillover estimates the probability that a high-traffic day blocks the street. ***Continuous cross-check:*** a fluid-flow approximation, dx/dt = λ(t) − ρ(x)/E[S(t)]. Here x is the expected number of cars at the bottleneck station, and ρ(x) inverts the Pollaczek–Khinchine M/G/1 mean. It is solved with Euler's method and fourth-order Runge–Kutta, with a step-size halving test, and compared with the DES queue profile at a demand level where a line forms. It gives only a mean trajectory, so it cannot replace the DES. A conceptual-model diagram accompanies the notebook (`figures/conceptual_model.png`). |
| **Part 4: measures, units, computation** | ***Average delay (seconds):*** mean over all cars of time in system minus the car's own activity time. ***Maximum queue (cars):*** the largest number of cars on the approach during the day, not counting the car at the stop. A car at the pre-check spot does count, since it occupies the approach. ***Guard utilization (fraction of on-duty time):*** total busy guard-time divided by total on-duty guard-time. This keeps configurations with 12, 14 and 15 guard-hours comparable. ***Spillover duration (minutes):*** total time during which the queue holds at least K cars, where K is the number of cars that fit on the approach before the tail reaches Pablo Ocampo Sr. Extension. ***Supplementary:*** average delay for cars arriving 07:00–10:00; 90th-percentile and longest delay; occupancy of the stop and reader; guard-hours per day; and the probability that a day has any spillover (share of replications, with a Wilson 95% interval). ***Headroom:*** from the stress test, the first demand multiple at which P(spillover day) ≥ 10%. Every measure is computed once per replication and reported as a mean with a 95% confidence interval. |
| **Part 5: inputs and where each value comes from** | The group has **no access to the gate or its records**. No timings, gate log or counts can be collected. Every campus input is therefore a documented estimate, kept with its basis and source in `data/input_parameters.csv`, which the code only reads. ***Arrival profile:*** a group estimate per 15-minute interval: about 214 cars 07:00–10:00, peaking at 100 cars/hour around 07:30, and about 402 per day (`data/arrival_rates.csv`). ***Sticker check C:*** lognormal, mean 4 s, SD 2 s; group estimate. ***ID tap and barrier T:*** lognormal, mean 4 s, SD 2 s. Group estimate, set above published card/RFID read times of about 1–3 s to allow for reaching out of the window and the barrier cycle. ***Move-up M:*** lognormal, mean 3 s, SD 1 s; estimate. ***Refusal R:*** lognormal, mean 45 s, SD 15 s. ***No-sticker share:*** 5% of arrivals. ***K:*** 8 cars, estimated. It can and should be measured on Google Maps / Street View as approach length ÷ (car length + gap). Lognormal parameters come from each stated mean and SD (method of moments). ***Real public data for the arrival assumption:*** per-vehicle entry times from the SFpark garage transactions (San Francisco Municipal Transportation Agency, 2011–2013, public evaluation data) test the *form* of assumption A1. The tests are chi-square uniformity within 15-minute intervals, and index-of-dispersion across days with a Bonferroni correction. They are never used to set campus volumes, because a downtown pay garage has no campus class-start peak. |
| **Part 5: assumptions** | Each is marked confirmed (C) or provisional (P); every provisional one is tested in Parts 8–10. **A1 (P)** Arrivals are Poisson, with a rate constant within each 15-minute interval (tested on SFpark data). **A2 (P)** Activity times are lognormal with the estimated mean and SD. **A3 (C)** One reader and one stop position; the car stops once for the check and the tap. **A4 (P)** Guard 2 pre-checks a car only while on duty and only when the stop is occupied. A pre-checked car keeps the pre-check spot until the stop frees (no buffer between them). **A5 (P)** Guard 2 finishes a check already started at closing time and starts no more. **A6 (P)** A refused car turns out of the lane after R without passing the reader. **A7 (P)** The reader never fails. **A8 (P)** Move-up time is part of every service, even with no line (conservative). **A9 (C, scope)** No balking, reneging, basement-capacity limit or exit interaction. **A10 (P)** The line is empty and the guards idle at 07:00. |
| **Part 6: tools** | Python 3.12, SimPy 4.1.2, NumPy, SciPy, pandas and matplotlib, in one Jupyter notebook that regenerates every table and figure from `data/`. It also runs in Google Colab after cloning the repository. `python parking_sim.py` gives a quick baseline without Jupyter. ***Random numbers:*** NumPy's default generator (PCG64), a tested long-period generator, rather than a hand-coded LCG. Each replication's seed is split with SeedSequence into one independent stream per random input: arrivals, sticker/no sticker, C, T, M and R. Arrivals are generated by inverting the cumulative rate function Λ(t) from unit-rate exponential gaps. Activity times are generated by the inverse transform X = F⁻¹(U). As a result, car i is the same car, with the same type and the same activity times, in every configuration and every demand scenario. This is **common random numbers**, so differences reflect the staffing change, not seed noise. All seeds are recorded (seeds 1 to n). |
| **Part 7: experiment design and output analysis** | Three configurations (A, B, C). Terminating runs from 07:00 until the line is cleared after 19:00, with no warm-up. ***Replications:*** a pilot of 30, then the Week 7 rule, n × (current half-width ÷ target half-width)², applied to every configuration and every paired difference. The targets are ±1 s mean delay, ±1 car maximum queue, ±1 min spillover and ±0.01 utilization. The **headline results use the resulting n**, and the pilot, the calculation and the final n are reported. For each configuration, each measure is reported as a mean with a 95% t confidence interval. Configurations are compared with **paired-t** 95% intervals on per-seed differences for B − A, C − A and C − B (the value of the extra hour). A Bonferroni-adjusted level (1 − 0.05/3 per interval) is also reported so the three conclusions hold jointly. Because common random numbers can make a fraction of a second statistically significant, each difference is also judged on whether its size matters next to the extra guard-hours. ***Stress test:*** demand ×1.0 to ×5.0 in steps of 0.5, for all three configurations. It reports the headroom, the first multiple at which P(spillover day) ≥ 10%, for every K from 5 to 12. |
| **Part 8: verification (did we build the model right?)** | Every check is kept in the notebook and rerun after each model change. **V1** Hand trace from the DES lecture (interarrivals 2, 4, 1, 3; services 3, 2, 4, 1): delays of 0, 0, 1, 2; Wq = 0.75; utilization = 10/13. **V2** Deterministic D/D/1 from Week 2: no delay. **V3** Conservation at 19:00: arrived = entered + refused + on the approach + at the stop. **V4** Sanity: delays ≥ 0, queue ≥ 0, utilizations in [0, 1]. **V5** Cars reach the stop in arrival order. **V6** Little's law: the area under the queue curve equals the total time cars spend on the approach. **V7** Guard 2 starts no check at or after closing time. **V8** Common random numbers: the same cars in every configuration and every demand scenario. **V9** Known answers (steady-state, with warm-up discarded): M/M/1, and M/G/1 on the model's own mixture of activity times against Pollaczek–Khinchine. **V10** Every car's delay equals the Lindley recursion on the same inputs. **V11** A two-station hand trace worked on paper: departures 5, 10, 15 with one guard and 5, 8, 11 with the pre-check. **V12** Equivalences: B with guard 2 closing at 07:00 equals A; B with zero check time and no refusals equals A. **V13** Every admitted car holds the stop for at least T + M. The fluid model (Part 3) gives an independent cross-check of the queue profile. |
| **Part 9: validation (did we build the right model?)** | No field observation of the gate is possible, so the strongest validation, comparing model output with counts of the real line, **cannot be done**. This is stated as the main limitation. The checks that remain: ***Historical / face validity:*** the group has never seen the line reach the street, so the model at estimated demand should give a low probability of a spillover day. This is not independent evidence, because the same group chose the inputs. ***Extreme conditions:*** near-zero demand gives near-zero delay; demand ×5 makes the line grow; a slow reader (tap ×10) makes it grow even at normal demand. ***Published ranges:*** the modelled time per sticker car at the stop is compared with published card/RFID (about 1–3 s) and ticket (8–15 s) transaction times. ***If access is ever granted:*** count the cars on the approach every 5 minutes from 07:05 to 10:00 on one morning, and compare each count with the 2.5th–97.5th percentile band of simulated days. |
| **Part 10: sensitivity analysis** | All three configurations are rerun (same seeds, common random numbers) for arrival demand ×0.8, ×1.0 and ×1.2 (low, expected, high), crossed with processing time ×0.8, ×1.0 and ×1.2. The study is also rerun with gamma and exponential activity times in place of lognormal, and spillover is recomputed for K from 5 to 12. The stress test (Part 7) extends demand to ×5. The recommendation is **robust** if the choice between A, B and C does not change across the plausible inputs, and the headroom statement says how far the estimates could be wrong before it would. Scaling demand keeps the day's shape, so a peak that starts later or lasts longer is not tested; that is reported as a limitation. |
| **Part 11: reporting** | The final report states each estimate with its interval and number of replications, the study type and run length, which verification and validation checks passed, and whether the recommendation survives the sensitivity analysis and stress test. It follows the Week 7 five kinds of evidence: precision, study design, verification, validation and sensitivity. Guard-hours appear next to every configuration's results. Limitations are reported explicitly: every campus input is estimated; validation without field data; the arrival test uses proxy data; K is estimated; the pre-check policy (A4); a uniform demand scaling that keeps the day's shape; and the exclusion of balking, reneging, reader failures and the basement. |
