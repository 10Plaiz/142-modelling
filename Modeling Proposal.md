| **The system in one line** | Vehicles arrive at the campus parking lot’s single entry booth, queue along the entry approach, are processed one at a time by an attendant who issues a ticket or checks an ID, and proceed into the lot once cleared. |
| **Part 1: problem and decision** | The lot is served by one entry booth staffed from 07:00 to 19:00. On high-traffic mornings the entry queue grows past the gate and onto Pablo Ocampo Sr. Extension, where waiting vehicles block a lane of through traffic and draw complaints from drivers and neighbouring establishments. Management must decide whether the cost of an additional attendant during the morning peak is justified by the reduction in queueing. The decision is: staff a second entry booth from 07:00 to 09:00 on high-traffic days, or keep a single booth for the whole day. ***Decision question:*** on a high-traffic day, does a second booth from 07:00 to 09:00 reduce spillover onto Pablo Ocampo Sr. Extension and average waiting time by a statistically significant margin, and does that result still hold when the input estimates are somewhat wrong? The model estimates the queueing benefit. Whether that benefit is worth an attendant’s wage is a management judgement that stays outside the model. ***Why simulation:*** arrivals peak and fall within two hours, so the queue never settles into the steady state that closed-form queueing formulas assume. Trialling a second booth on the real gate would disrupt traffic and cost wages before the answer is known. |
| **Part 2: entities** | Vehicles arriving to enter the lot. One vehicle is one entity, for example, a faculty car that arrives at 07:42, waits behind six other vehicles, is issued a ticket, and enters. Each vehicle carries two attributes: its arrival time and its processing time. |
| **Part 2: resources** | Entry booths, each processing one vehicle at a time. Baseline configuration: one booth, open 07:00–19:00. Alternative configuration: a second booth open 07:00–09:00, then one booth for the remainder of the day. |
| **Part 2: arrivals** | Vehicles arrive one at a time across the 12-hour operating day. Arrivals are modelled as a Poisson process whose rate is piecewise constant over 15-minute intervals, so the 07:00–09:00 morning peak is represented by a higher arrival rate in those intervals rather than by a separate mechanism. |
| **Part 2: run start and stop** | A run is one operating day, 07:00 to 19:00. Arrivals stop at 19:00; vehicles already in the queue at that point are processed before the run ends. Each run starts with an empty queue and an idle booth. This is a **terminating** study: the day has a natural start and end, and the empty 07:00 start is the real condition being studied, so no warm-up period is discarded. |
| **Part 2: out of scope** | 1\. Parking space availability inside the lot where every cleared vehicle is assumed to find a slot. 2\. The exit booth and departure traffic. 3\. Payment disputes, lost tickets, and manual supervisor overrides. 4\. Balking (drivers who see the line and go elsewhere) and reneging (drivers who join the queue then leave) every arriving vehicle joins the queue and stays until processed. |
| **Part 3: model type** | Discrete-event simulation, built in the process-interaction world view (SimPy maintains the future event list). A continuous fluid-flow model is built alongside it, only as a cross-check (see below). |
| **Part 3: why this type fits** | ***Classification:*** the model is **dynamic** because the order and timing of arrivals determine how long each vehicle waits. It is **stochastic** because interarrival and processing times vary from vehicle to vehicle, so the same day configuration gives different outcomes on each run. It is **discrete** because its state changes only at events. The state variables are the number of vehicles waiting in the queue and the busy-or-idle and open-or-closed status of each booth. Both change only at discrete instants and hold constant in between, so there is no continuously varying level to integrate. The event set is: vehicle arrival, processing start, processing completion, and the closing of the second booth at 09:00 in the two-booth configuration. Processing time is an *activity* the model samples. Queue waiting time is a *delay* the model produces as output and never samples. ***Monte Carlo element:*** each replication is one sampled high-traffic day. The share of simulated days with any spillover is a Monte Carlo estimate of the probability that a high-traffic day blocks the road. ***Continuous cross-check:*** the same inputs drive a fluid-flow approximation, dx/dt = λ(t) − c(t)·ρ(x)/E[S]. Here x is the expected number of vehicles in the system, and ρ(x) comes from inverting the steady-state M/G/c mean queue length (Pollaczek–Khinchine for one booth). A deterministic version, in which booths drain at full rate whenever there is a backlog, is also run. Both are solved with Euler’s method and fourth-order Runge–Kutta, with a step-size halving test. This model only produces a mean trajectory, so it cannot give maximum queue length, spillover probability, or day-to-day spread. It is used only to check that the DES queue profile agrees with an independently derived one, and to show how much congestion comes from variability alone. |
| **Part 4: measures, units, computation** | ***Average waiting time (minutes):*** mean across all vehicles of processing-start time minus arrival time. ***Maximum queue length (vehicles):*** running maximum of the number of vehicles waiting, not counting any vehicle at a booth, sampled at every event. ***Booth utilization (fraction of staffed time):*** total busy booth-time divided by total booth-time staffed, so that the one-booth and two-booth configurations remain comparable even though the second booth is open for only two hours. A second booth still serving a vehicle at 09:00 finishes it, and its staffed time extends to that finish. ***Spillover duration (minutes):*** total simulated time during which the waiting queue holds at least K vehicles, where K is the number of vehicles the entry approach can hold before the tail of the queue reaches Pablo Ocampo Sr. Extension. ***Supplementary:*** 90th-percentile wait, booth utilization within 07:00–09:00, and the probability that a high-traffic day has any spillover (share of replications, with a Wilson 95% interval). Every measure is computed once per replication and reported as a mean with a 95% confidence interval across replications. |
| **Part 5: inputs and where each value comes from** | ***Arrival counts per 15-minute interval:*** taken from the main gate entry log for one full high-traffic operating day, using the recorded entry timestamps to reconstruct arrivals per interval. The log timestamps a vehicle when it *passes the booth*, not when it joins the line. While a queue exists, passages per interval are capped by booth capacity, so the log under-counts peak demand. To correct this, one member will stand at the tail of the queue from 07:00 to 09:00 and tally vehicles *joining* the line per 15-minute interval. Those tallies replace the log counts for the peak intervals. Whether any vehicles are already waiting at 07:00 will also be recorded. ***Per-vehicle processing time:*** stopwatch timing, from the vehicle stopping at the booth to the barrier lifting, of 30 vehicles during the 07:00–09:00 peak window and 30 vehicles off-peak. A Mann–Whitney U test at 5% decides whether the two windows need separate distributions or can be pooled. Exponential, lognormal, and triangular distributions will be fitted to the sample by maximum likelihood. The chosen fit is the one with the lowest AIC among those not rejected by a chi-square test (equiprobable bins, 5% level). Kolmogorov–Smirnov results are also reported, and no distribution is assumed in advance. With 30 to 60 observations these tests have low power, so the choice is also checked by sensitivity analysis (Part 10). ***Approach capacity K (vehicles):*** measured on site before the model is built, as the usable approach length divided by average vehicle length plus inter-vehicle gap, rounded down. ***Booth count and staffing hours:*** campus policy, known exactly (current = 1 booth, 07:00–19:00; alternative = 2 booths during the peak window). ***Validation data (never used for fitting):*** the number of vehicles waiting, counted every 5 minutes from 07:05 to 09:00, preferably on a different high-traffic day from the arrival data. |
| **Part 5: assumptions** | Each assumption is marked as confirmed (C) or provisional (P), and every provisional one is tested in Part 9 or Part 10. **A1 (P)** Arrivals are Poisson, with a rate that is constant within each 15-minute interval. **A2 (P)** Processing time follows the fitted distribution for the window in which the vehicle arrives. **A3 (P)** In the two-booth configuration, one shared queue feeds whichever booth frees first. This is the best case: two separate lanes chosen by drivers would perform somewhat worse. **A4 (P)** At 09:00 the second booth finishes its current vehicle and then accepts no more (a non-preemptive close). **A5 (P)** The second booth draws processing times from the same fitted distribution as the first. **A6 (C, scope)** No balking, reneging, parking-space limit, or exit interaction (Part 2). **A7 (P)** The queue is empty and the booth idle at 07:00. **A8 (P)** Spillover begins when the waiting queue, not counting the vehicle at the booth, reaches K. |
| **Part 6: tools** | Python 3.12, SimPy 4.1.2, NumPy, and SciPy for the model and the distribution fitting; pandas for summarising the observed input data; matplotlib for figures; all in one Jupyter notebook that regenerates every table and figure from the input files. ***Random numbers:*** NumPy’s default generator (PCG64), a tested long-period generator, rather than a hand-coded LCG. Each replication’s seed is split with SeedSequence into two independent streams, one for arrivals and one for processing times. Interarrival gaps are generated as X = −(1/λ)·ln(1 − U) within each interval, and the gap restarts at each interval boundary, which is exact because the exponential distribution is memoryless. Each vehicle’s processing time is generated by the inverse transform X = F⁻¹(U). Giving every vehicle its own U means the one-booth and two-booth runs see identical vehicles: this is **common random numbers**, so the difference between them reflects the staffing change rather than seed noise. All random seeds are recorded (seeds 1–30 for the main experiment). |
| **Part 7: experiment design and output analysis** | Two configurations (1 booth all day; 2 booths 07:00–09:00), 30 replications each. The study is terminating, so each run lasts from 07:00 until the queue is cleared after 19:00, with no warm-up. For each configuration, each measure is reported as a mean with a 95% t confidence interval. Because of common random numbers, the configurations are compared with a **paired-t** 95% confidence interval on the per-seed difference (1 booth − 2 booths). The difference is significant if that interval excludes zero. ***Were 30 replications enough?*** The required number is n × (current half-width ÷ target half-width)², rounded up. The targets are ±0.25 min for waiting time, ±5 min for spillover, ±2 vehicles for maximum queue, and ±0.02 for utilization. Where 30 falls short, the follow-up number of replications is run, and the achieved interval is checked again. |
| **Part 8: verification (did we build the model right?)** | Every check below is kept in the notebook and rerun after each model change. **V1** Hand trace from the DES lecture (interarrivals 2, 4, 1, 3; services 3, 2, 4, 1): delays of 0, 0, 1, 2; Wq = 0.75; utilization = 10/13. **V2** Deterministic D/D/1 from Week 2 (arrival every 1.00 min, service 0.99 min): no waiting. **V3** Conservation at 19:00: arrived = finished + waiting + in service, in every run. **V4** Sanity: waits ≥ 0, queue ≥ 0, utilization in [0, 1]. **V5** Service starts in first-come-first-served order. **V6** Little’s law: the area under the queue-length curve equals the sum of delays. **V7** The second booth starts no vehicle after 09:00. **V8** Common random numbers: both configurations receive identical arrivals and processing times. **V9** Known answers, run as a separate steady-state test with warm-up discarded: M/M/1 and M/M/2 (Erlang C) mean delay against theory. The fluid-flow model (Part 3) provides an additional, independent cross-check of the queue profile. |
| **Part 9: validation (did we build the right model?)** | ***Comparison with real data:*** the baseline one-booth model is compared with the queue-length snapshots (Part 5). The observed day is a single realization, so each snapshot is checked against the 2.5th–97.5th percentile band of simulated days at that time, not against the confidence interval of the mean. Snapshots inside the band count as “no evidence the model is wrong”, never as proof that it is right. ***Extreme conditions:*** at 5% of normal traffic the wait should be near zero, and at 150% the queue and spillover should grow. ***Face validity:*** the queue-over-time profile will be reviewed with the booth attendants or parking staff. Input data are never reused for validation. |
| **Part 10: sensitivity analysis** | Because the arrival rates come from one day and the processing times from 60 timings, the study is rerun (30 replications each, with common random numbers) across a grid of arrival rate × 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, 1.2 and processing time × 0.85, 1.0, 1.15. It is also rerun under each candidate processing-time distribution, and with K − 1 and K + 1. The recommendation is **robust** if the second booth reduces spillover significantly across the plausible inputs, or if spillover is negligible in both configurations. |
| **Part 11: reporting** | The final report states the estimate with its interval and number of replications, the study type and run length, which verification and validation checks passed, and whether the recommendation survives the sensitivity analysis. It follows the five kinds of evidence from Week 7: precision, study design, verification, validation, and sensitivity. Limitations are reported explicitly: single-day arrival data, gate-log censoring if the tally is incomplete, the shared-queue assumption (A3), the empty start (A7), and the exclusion of balking and reneging. |
